## Purpose of the Paper

Weinberg's starting point is methodological. Quantum mechanics is central to modern
physics, but unlike relativity or the Standard Model, it is rarely tested as a general
framework at very high precision. Tests of Bell inequalities rule out local hidden
variables, but they are not high-precision tests of the exact linear structure of
quantum mechanics itself.

His question is:

> Can we construct a logically coherent nonlinear generalization of quantum mechanics
> that reduces to ordinary quantum mechanics when nonlinear parameters are small, and
> that can guide precision experiments?

The goal is not to argue that quantum mechanics is likely wrong. The goal is to build
a controlled foil: a family of nonlinear theories that preserve enough of the ordinary
formalism to be physically interpretable and experimentally testable.

The paper's abstract captures the program:

- introduce nonlinear corrections to ordinary quantum mechanics;
- keep Hamiltonian-type time evolution;
- keep the rule that wavefunctions differing by an overall complex factor represent
  the same physical state;
- provide a way to combine separated subsystems;
- define eigenvalues and expectation values;
- analyze probabilities through measurement models rather than assuming the usual
  Born-rule machinery survives unchanged;
- identify experimental signatures in spin precession, Stern-Gerlach-type experiments,
  and spectral-line shifts or broadening.

## Section 2: Formalism

### Wavefunctions and Rays

As in ordinary quantum mechanics, the state is represented by a complex wavefunction.
For most of the formal discussion, Weinberg uses a finite-dimensional wavefunction
`psi_k`, with `k = 1, ..., N`.

The crucial ray condition is retained:

```text
psi and Z psi represent the same physical state
```

for any nonzero complex number `Z`.

This requirement drives the homogeneity constraints throughout the paper.

### Observables as Homogeneous Functions

In ordinary quantum mechanics, an observable is represented by a Hermitian matrix
`A_kl`, or equivalently by the bilinear function:

```text
psi*_k A_kl psi_l
```

Weinberg generalizes this by allowing observables to be real functions:

```text
a(psi, psi*)
```

not necessarily bilinear, but homogeneous of degree one in `psi` and degree one in
`psi*`. This ensures that the associated expectation-like quantity is insensitive to
overall scaling of the wavefunction.

He defines a product of such functions by a derivative operation that reduces to
matrix multiplication in the bilinear case. This product is distributive but generally
neither commutative nor associative. The failure of associativity is one of the main
places where the generalized theory departs from ordinary quantum mechanics.

### Symmetries and the Generalized Commutator

Weinberg defines infinitesimal transformations generated by these homogeneous
functions. The corresponding bracket is essentially a classical Poisson bracket on
the real and imaginary parts of the wavefunction.

Important properties:

- the bracket is antisymmetric;
- it satisfies the Jacobi identity;
- it supports Lie algebras of symmetry generators;
- the norm is invariant under all such transformations.

This is why the theory is Hamiltonian in structure even though the wavefunction
evolution is nonlinear.

### Time Dependence

Time translation is generated by a real Hamiltonian function:

```text
h(psi, psi*)
```

The nonlinear Schrodinger equation is:

```text
i d psi_k / dt = partial h / partial psi*_k
```

When `h` is bilinear, this is ordinary quantum mechanics. When `h` has higher-order
homogeneous dependence on the state, the theory becomes nonlinear.

The dynamics is a classical Hamiltonian system on the real variables:

```text
Re psi_k, Im psi_k
```

The conserved quantities always include:

- the norm `n`;
- the Hamiltonian function `h`.

But because the generalized product is non-associative, products of conserved
functions need not themselves be conserved. This means nonlinear systems with
`N > 2` need not be integrable.

### Quasi-Periodicity and Chaos

Ordinary finite-dimensional quantum mechanics always gives quasi-periodic time
dependence: a sum of finitely many oscillatory terms with fixed frequencies.

Weinberg emphasizes that nonlinear Hamiltonian systems may behave differently:

- some remain quasi-periodic if integrable;
- near-integrable systems often remain quasi-periodic for most initial conditions;
- chaotic wavefunction evolution becomes possible in principle.

He discusses the averaging principle, KAM theorem, and Nekhoroshev theorem as tools
for understanding why small nonlinearities may still produce motion that behaves
approximately as if integrable, except near resonances.

### Galilean Invariance and One-Particle Systems

Weinberg then asks how to preserve Galilean invariance. For a single nonrelativistic
particle, he keeps the conventional bilinear generators for:

- momentum;
- boosts;
- angular momentum;
- mass/norm.

The Hamiltonian may then include the ordinary kinetic term plus a nonlinear internal
energy function. For particles with spin or internal degrees of freedom, these
nonlinear internal terms can be nontrivial while preserving Galilean invariance.

This is important: Weinberg's nonlinear corrections are not arbitrary modifications
of the spatial Schrodinger equation. They are homogeneous nonlinearities in internal
degrees of freedom, designed so that `psi` and `Z psi` remain the same physical state.

### Local and Nonlocal Options

Weinberg discusses two possible forms for nonlinear internal energy.

One is local in position: the Hamiltonian density depends on the local spinor
components and their conjugates, with homogeneity enforced locally.

Another option uses global powers of the norm to maintain homogeneity. This avoids
some local singularities but makes the theory nonlocal, because the evolution in one
region depends on the global norm integrated over all space.

Weinberg notes a striking many-worlds consequence of such nonlocal theories: if the
wavefunction of the universe is spread over an enormous number of branches, the
nonlinear effect in any one branch could be diluted to practical unobservability. This
does **not** apply to the local class he mainly wants to test.

### Separated Systems

A major formal requirement is that separated, noninteracting systems must combine
without introducing unphysical correlations.

For systems I and II, with wavefunctions `psi_k` and `phi_l`, the combined system has
wavefunction:

```text
Psi_kl
```

For noninteracting systems, Weinberg defines the combined Hamiltonian so that, when
the joint state factorizes,

```text
Psi_kl = psi_k phi_l
```

the separate factors obey their separate nonlinear Schrodinger equations.

This is Weinberg's proposed solution to the problem of combining separated systems in
a nonlinear theory. It is also exactly the area where later criticism enters: in the
note added in proof, Weinberg acknowledges Polchinski's concern that this treatment
may permit EPR-type causality violations.

### Canonical Changes of Basis

Weinberg generalizes unitary changes of basis to homogeneous canonical
transformations. These preserve the generalized commutator structure and the norm.

This enlarged transformation freedom is used later to put simple systems, especially
two-component systems, into convenient canonical forms.

### Two-Component Example

For a two-component system, Weinberg shows that the Hamiltonian can be put in the form:

```text
h = n hbar(a)
```

where:

```text
a = |psi_2|^2 / n
```

Ordinary quantum mechanics corresponds to `hbar(a)` being linear in `a`. Nonlinearity
means `hbar(a)` is nonlinear, and then the oscillation frequencies depend on the
initial condition `a`. This initial-state dependence of characteristic frequencies is
one of the paper's central experimental signatures.

## Section 3: Eigenvalues

Weinberg generalizes the eigenvalue condition.

For an observable represented by a function `a(psi, psi*)`, a state has definite value
`alpha` when:

```text
partial a / partial psi*_k = alpha psi_k
```

and the complex-conjugate condition also holds. In the bilinear case, this reduces to
the ordinary matrix eigenvalue equation.

The eigenvalue can be written:

```text
alpha = a(psi, psi*) / n
```

for the eigenstate.

Eigenstates are stationary points of:

```text
a(psi, psi*) / n
```

on projective Hilbert space. Weinberg uses Morse theory to argue that an observable
has at least `N` eigenvectors for an `N`-component wavefunction. If the nonlinear
correction is small, one expects the same number of eigenvectors as in ordinary
quantum mechanics.

For weak perturbations, the first-order eigenvalue shift is simply the nonlinear
perturbation evaluated on the unperturbed eigenvector, analogous to first-order
perturbation theory in ordinary quantum mechanics.

For the two-component example, sufficiently strong nonlinearities can generate extra
stationary points, hence additional energy eigenstates. For weak nonlinearities, only
the usual two remain.

## Section 4: Spinning Particles in External Fields

Weinberg then turns to experimentally interesting systems: spinning particles in weak
external fields. The theme is that nonlinearities can violate consequences of symmetry
that, in ordinary quantum mechanics, depend on both symmetry and linearity.

### Spin 1/2

For a spin-1/2 particle in a uniform magnetic field, Weinberg finds no nonlinear
correction in the internal dynamics. The most general Hamiltonian with the right
properties is bilinear.

This means pointlike spin-1/2 particles such as electrons, protons, and neutrons do
not show Weinberg-type nonlinear effects in weak uniform magnetic fields, at least in
this simplified treatment.

But a spin-1/2 particle in a quadrupole electric field can have an interaction that
ordinary linear quantum mechanics would forbid at first order. This is an example of
a symmetry-based null prediction of ordinary quantum mechanics becoming testable.

### Spin 1

For a spin-1 particle in a weak uniform magnetic field, nonlinear internal energy terms
can exist.

Consequences:

- energy levels need not obey the equal-spacing rule;
- characteristic precession frequencies can depend on the initial wavefunction;
- additional oscillation frequencies can appear that vanish in ordinary quantum
  mechanics.

This is important experimentally because spin-precession frequencies can be measured
with very high precision.

### Spin 3/2

Weinberg analyzes a spin-3/2 particle with a simple nonlinear Hamiltonian proportional
to `j^2/n`, plus a weak magnetic field.

Again, nonlinear effects show up as:

- departures from equal spacing of energy levels;
- characteristic frequencies depending on initial conditions;
- possible additional degenerate energy eigenvalues above a threshold in the
  nonlinear parameter.

He then considers more realistic spin-precession experiments, where additional
environmental fields from walls or apparatus introduce terms that make the system not
strictly integrable. Treating the nonlinear piece as a small perturbation and using
averaging methods, he finds frequency shifts depending on the initial state's action
variables. This dependence is a direct signature of nonlinear quantum mechanics.

Weinberg mentions proposed measurements by Norman Fortson and Thomas Chupp involving
spin-precession experiments on nuclei such as mercury-201 and neon-21.

## Section 5: Probabilities and Expectation Values

Weinberg is careful here: the probabilistic interpretation does not carry over
automatically.

In ordinary quantum mechanics, the Born rule can be stated in terms of projections
onto eigenvectors. But in the nonlinear theory:

- the measurement of a general observable may yield possible values depending on the
  wavefunction;
- powers of an observable are ambiguous because the generalized product is
  non-associative;
- therefore one cannot simply reconstruct a probability distribution from expectation
  values of powers as in ordinary quantum mechanics.

Instead, Weinberg proposes a more modest postulate:

For commuting observables represented by homogeneous real functions `a`, `b`, ...,
their measured average values in state `psi` are:

```text
<A> = a(psi, psi*) / n
<B> = b(psi, psi*) / n
```

This has several desirable features:

- it is invariant under rescaling `psi -> Z psi`;
- it is additive for separated uncorrelated systems;
- it reproduces eigenvalues in eigenstates;
- expectation values lie between the maximum and minimum eigenvalues.

For observables protected by symmetry and represented bilinearly, such as angular
momentum, the usual probability interpretation can still be recovered.

### Stern-Gerlach-Type Measurements

Weinberg uses Stern-Gerlach experiments as a model for measuring a general nonlinear
observable.

If an observable `A` is coupled to an external field gradient, the beam splits not
necessarily into a finite set of eigenvalue beams, but generally into infinitely many
sub-beams corresponding to integer combinations of characteristic frequencies of the
nonlinear dynamics.

The weighted average over these outcomes reproduces the expectation value `a/n`, as
his postulate requires. This shows that his expectation-value rule can be operationally
consistent even when the probability distribution is more complicated than in ordinary
quantum mechanics.

## Section 6: Spectral Lines

This section analyzes absorption frequencies and line shapes.

In ordinary quantum mechanics, spectral lines occur at differences of energy
eigenvalues, almost independently of experimental details.

Weinberg shows this is no longer generally true in nonlinear quantum mechanics.

### Single Photon, Initial Energy Eigenstate

In the simplest case, where one photon interacts with a two-level system initially in
an energy eigenstate, the resonant frequency is still:

```text
omega = E_2 - E_1
```

as in ordinary quantum mechanics.

### Classical Field / Many-Photon Limit

For a classical oscillating electromagnetic field, the resonance condition instead
depends on characteristic frequencies of the nonlinear time evolution:

```text
omega = omega_2(a) - omega_1(a)
```

where `a` encodes the initial mixture of the two components of the wavefunction.

These characteristic frequencies need not equal the difference of energy eigenvalues.
Weinberg shows that the energy difference is an average over these frequency
differences, not generally equal to any particular one.

### Experimental Consequences

There are two main effects.

1. **Line broadening from initial-state variation**

   If atoms or nuclei in a sample have different initial wavefunctions, they can have
   different characteristic resonance frequencies. This produces anomalous broadening
   of absorption lines.

2. **Detuning during the transition**

   As the system evolves under the driving field, the state changes, and therefore the
   characteristic transition frequency changes. A weak field may start driving a
   transition but then detune itself as the wavefunction moves through state space.

For a simple quadratic nonlinear correction, Weinberg derives a condition: if the
driving field is too weak compared with the nonlinear parameter, the transition cannot
proceed all the way from one energy eigenstate to the other.

This gives a way to bound nonlinear corrections experimentally.

He cites work on hyperfine transitions in beryllium-9 ions, already used to set very
strong bounds on nonlinear corrections to quantum mechanics. At the time of the paper,
he notes a bound improved to roughly:

```text
|epsilon| < 2.4 x 10^-20 eV
```

which he describes as the most stringent current limit known to him.

## Appendix: Useful Transformation and KAM Discussion

The appendix introduces variables adapted to projective Hilbert space:

- the norm `n`;
- an overall phase;
- reduced complex coordinates `z_l` describing ratios of wavefunction components.

In these variables, energy eigenstates become fixed points of the reduced Hamiltonian
system.

If the reduced system is integrable, Weinberg shows one can perform a homogeneous
canonical transformation so that the Hamiltonian depends only on moduli of new
wavefunction components.

For nonintegrable but nearly linear systems, he invokes Birkhoff normal forms and KAM
ideas. The upshot is cautious:

- near energy eigenstates, small nonlinear perturbations often still allow
  quasi-periodic behavior;
- globally, the relevant KAM non-degeneracy assumptions are not automatically
  satisfied;
- numerical evidence suggests near-integrable behavior may persist more broadly than
  theorems alone guarantee.

This appendix matters because Weinberg's experimental predictions often assume that
the nonlinear dynamics behaves quasi-periodically enough to define characteristic
frequencies.

## Note Added in Proof: Polchinski's Causality Objection

The note added in proof is historically important.

Weinberg writes that Joseph Polchinski, in private communication, raised the question
whether nonlinear generalizations of quantum mechanics allow causality violations in
Einstein-Podolsky-Rosen-type experiments.

Weinberg says this appears to be a serious problem because, with the treatment of
separated systems used in Section 2(g), functions representing observables associated
with separated systems do not necessarily commute.

He adds that work is in progress by Polchinski and himself on a treatment of separated
systems that would avoid the problem.

This note is the direct bridge from Weinberg 1989 to Polchinski 1991.

For your talk, this is important:

> Weinberg himself recognized, before publication, that Polchinski's EPR objection was
> a serious challenge to the separated-systems part of the formalism.

## What Weinberg Achieved

Weinberg did three things that matter for the later Hsu/Polchinski/Diosi line.

1. **He made nonlinear quantum mechanics precise enough to analyze.**

   Before this, "nonlinear quantum mechanics" could mean many things. Weinberg gave a
   Hamiltonian, homogeneous, ray-based framework close enough to ordinary quantum
   mechanics to support eigenvalues, symmetries, separated systems, and experimental
   predictions.

2. **He identified experimental signatures.**

   The most important are:

   - initial-state-dependent transition or precession frequencies;
   - anomalous spectral-line broadening;
   - detuning under weak driving fields;
   - violations of ordinary equal-spacing rules that depend on linearity as well as
     symmetry.

3. **He exposed the deep problem later sharpened by Polchinski.**

   Combining nonlinear dynamics with separated entangled systems is not harmless.
   Weinberg's formalism tries to preserve separability, but the note added in proof
   acknowledges the EPR/causality danger that became Polchinski's 1991 result.

## Relation to Polchinski, Hsu, and Diosi

The chain is:

- **Weinberg 1989**: constructs a systematic nonlinear, Hamiltonian, ray-based
  generalization of quantum mechanics and proposes precision tests.
- **Polchinski 1991**: shows that Weinberg's framework faces a causality dilemma:
  either EPR signaling or communication between branches of the wavefunction.
- **Hsu 2025/2026**: argues that, in relativistic QFT using Tomonaga-Schwinger
  evolution, state-dependent nonlinear modifications generically fail foliation
  independence and therefore relativistic covariance.
- **Diosi 2026**: disputes Hsu's covariance diagnosis, arguing that the
  Tomonaga-Schwinger equation can remain covariant while the causality problem remains
  a Polchinski/Gisin-type operational signaling problem.

The safe talk formulation:

> Weinberg supplied the controlled nonlinear framework. Polchinski showed that its
> treatment of entangled separated systems creates a no-signaling crisis. Hsu argues
> that, in QFT language, the same crisis appears as failure of Tomonaga-Schwinger
> foliation independence. Diosi agrees that nonlinear QM has causality problems, but
> disputes that this implies failure of TS covariance.

## One-Paragraph Talk Version

Weinberg's 1989 paper is not a speculative claim that quantum mechanics is probably
wrong. It is a systematic attempt to build a controlled nonlinear generalization of
quantum mechanics so that exact linearity can be experimentally tested. He keeps states
as rays in Hilbert space and keeps Hamiltonian time evolution, but replaces linear
operators with homogeneous real functions of the wavefunction and its complex conjugate.
Ordinary quantum mechanics is recovered when the Hamiltonian function is quadratic;
nonlinear corrections make characteristic frequencies depend on the initial state.
This leads to possible experimental signatures: anomalous spin-precession frequencies,
violations of equal-spacing rules, spectral-line broadening, and detuning of driven
transitions. The paper also tries to define eigenvalues, expectation values,
probabilities, and separated systems in the nonlinear setting. Its historical
importance is that it made nonlinear quantum mechanics precise enough for Polchinski
to show, two years later, that the treatment of entangled separated systems leads to
superluminal signaling or related pathologies.
