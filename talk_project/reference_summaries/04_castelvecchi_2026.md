## The headline case: Erdős problem #1196

Liam Price — no university education, working from his home in southwest England —
solved Erdős problem #1196 using ChatGPT. The problem was posed by Paul Erdős in
1966 and had resisted specialists for 60 years.

**What surprised mathematicians**: Prior attempts rephrased the problem into
probability theory language (the natural move for trained number theorists). ChatGPT
solved it in the original number-theoretic formulation — and yet its solution
implicitly established the probability connection that human solvers had needed to
introduce explicitly at the start.

Terence Tao's commentary: GPT found a path through the original terrain that humans
had walked around, precisely because human training told them to take the detour.

Stanford mathematician Jared Duker Lichtman: like AI discovering a chess opening
"no one had thought of before because of human aesthetics and convention."

Published paper: Alexeev, B. et al. Preprint at arXiv doi.org/q6p7 (2026).

## Proof indigestion (Tao)

Not "AI is writing proofs." Not "AI is getting results wrong."

The concern: *we may soon have more verified results than the community can absorb
and understand.* Human referees are stretched thin evaluating human-written papers;
AI-generated ones make the problem worse.

A referee cannot quickly distinguish a correct AI proof from a plausible-but-wrong
one without doing the work of verification. Tao's hypothetical: a solved problem
autonomously verified but no human expert can give a talk on it or answer questions.

## AI slop (Williams, Harvard)

Lauren Williams: "AI models can be capable of producing something that looks pretty
convincing, and it takes a lot of time to figure out if there is a mistake."
Journal editors report horror stories of AI-generated mathematical content flooding
submission pipelines.

## The Litt position (most useful for a physics audience)

Daniel Litt (University of Toronto) — calibrated skeptic, not reflexive dismisser.
Calls the Erdős result "reasonably interesting, **unlike previous examples in recent
months**" — meaning there is now a backdrop of AI math results even a skeptic finds
underwhelming.

On future potential: Litt is puzzled that AI systems are **not already doing more**.
"Their knowledge of existing mathematics is superhuman and they have shown strong
reasoning capabilities. Plus, they don't get tired or demotivated."

Conclusion: "Part of the mystery is, we don't know what makes a human mathematician
good at math. It is unclear whether humans have some 'secret sauce' that makes them
uniquely creative." — this uncertainty cuts both ways.

## General-purpose models

"In many cases, advances have come from systems that are based on general-purpose
large language models (LLMs), such as GPT, Gemini and Claude, without any special
mathematical training."

Note: the speaker is a daily Claude user. This is not a third-party observation —
it is the context in which the talk exists.

## Bubeck (OpenAI)

Systems "still mostly rehashing techniques they absorbed from the existing
literature" — but mathematicians "have started to spot glimpses of original 'thought'
in the models' outputs — with the tools making surprising connections between
subfields." Structurally parallel to Hsu's account of GPT-5 and the TS suggestion.
